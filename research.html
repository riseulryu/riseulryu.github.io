Interest

The common problem

My research sits at the intersection of machine learning, biometric signal processing and digital health, with applications in authentication and mental health monitoring. These domains are connected by a common challenge: behavioural and biometric signals are not static. They change across time, devices, contexts and populations, and they change with mood and mental state.

That observation connects the authentication work I have completed to the mental health detection work I am now developing. Both are problems of modelling a signal that shifts, and the adaptation methods I built for authentication are applicable to the clinical detection setting.

Two strandsMy published record spans security and authentication, and computing education research.

Doctorate

The doctoral work

My thesis, Dynamic Adaptations in Multimodal Biometric Authentication (University of Tasmania, 2026), investigated how a multimodal biometric system with an adaptive approach can improve authentication accuracy over time. The theoretical foundation is that a person's biometric data is not a fixed template but a distribution that changes with time, context and device, so static models that treat the template as fixed will degrade in performance as the distribution drifts.

The thesis progressed through four stages. A systematic review established the state of the art in adaptive multimodal biometric authentication and identified the lack of adaptation mechanisms as a key limitation in existing systems (outputs 5, 7, 3). A multimodal authentication framework combining facial data and keystroke dynamics then demonstrated improved performance through quality-based fusion. Adaptive template and threshold update mechanisms were investigated next, revealing that generalised adaptation strategies were insufficient for handling individual differences in behavioural change (output 1). Finally, drawing on biometric zoo theory, the research introduced user-dependent adaptation strategies tailored to distinct categories of biometric variability, resulting in significant performance improvements over both non-adaptive and generalised adaptive approaches.

The thesis produced five first-author peer-reviewed publications in IEEE Access, ICT Express and Lecture Notes in Computer Science. A journal paper currently under review further extends the user-dependent adaptation framework towards personalised adaptation methods.

Current project

Current project: a systematic review of explainable AI, cybersecurity and sustainability

I am currently completing a systematic review of how explainable AI contributes to cybersecurity and sustainability. I designed the search strategy across Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library, screened retrieved records against explicit inclusion criteria, and am applying a quality assessment instrument to the included studies.

The review connects my authentication and machine learning security work to my earlier research on blockchain-based renewable energy trading in small local communities (output 9) and my Master’s feasibility study on blockchain for microgrid systems. It maps the evidence base in this area and identifies the gaps that a grant application would need to name.

Next

Adaptive digital mental health

My work on biometric data change motivated a direct question: whether the same biometric data, facial and keystroke dynamics, can serve as indicators for detecting and monitoring mental health conditions, given that a person's biometric data also changes with their mental state. Emerging evidence suggests that changes in typing behaviour, interaction patterns and facial expressions contain clinically relevant information associated with depression and other mental health conditions.

Despite rapid advances in machine learning for mental health prediction, significant challenges remain regarding model robustness, fairness, external validity and cross-population generalisation. Existing models often perform well within a single dataset but experience substantial performance degradation when transferred to new populations, languages or collection settings. This problem of domain shift closely mirrors the adaptation challenges addressed throughout my doctoral research.

Contribution

For computer science, this research will contribute novel adaptation and generalisation methods for machine learning systems operating in dynamic environments, with applications beyond mental health including fraud detection, cybersecurity and motor rehabilitation. For healthcare, it aims to develop adaptive digital biomarkers capable of supporting continuous and unobtrusive monitoring through devices people already use, with the potential to identify behavioural changes associated with depression earlier than conventional episodic screening. For the broader community, particularly young people and families who are a focus of Australia's current mental health investment, the goal is systems that are equitable across diverse populations rather than ones that perform well only for the majority population.
