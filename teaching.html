Philosophy

Build something that runs, then account for why it runs

Students learn information technology by making something that runs and then being asked to account for why it runs. Those are two different skills and the second is where the learning sits, so I assess the explaining rather than only the artefact.

Two things shape how I design a unit. The first is constructive alignment: outcomes, activities and assessment written as one object rather than three documents that cite each other. The second is active learning in science and engineering. In practice that means live demonstration worked in front of the class rather than pre-recorded, short applied tasks in every session, and project work that runs across several weeks.

I also have my own data rather than only a reading of other people's. A study I co-authored found that project-based collaborative learning improved programming performance in an introductory cohort (Yeom, Herbert & Ryu, 2022). A later one with the same group looked at how collaborative learning interacts with gender disparity in IT education, which changed how I compose project teams and what a group task credits individually (Yeom, Herbert & Ryu, 2025).

Assessment

Assessment, and the problem I have spent five years on

With ongoing technological advancements and the growing use of Generative AI (GenAI), it is essential to uphold academic integrity and ensure that students genuinely engage with the learning process.

What is left is structural. Secure a smaller number of points where a student's own capability is verified directly — supervised work, demonstration, an oral account of code they submitted — and open the rest of the assessment deliberately, with the tool use as the object of study rather than a leak to be plugged. That is the direction of the TEQSA guiding principles (Lodge et al., 2023), and it depends on teaching students to appraise the quality of an output they did not write.

05 / 6
Students

Students

I teach international and culturally diverse cohorts, and have since 2019. Most of what makes a unit work for those students is unglamorous. Predictable structure. Feedback that arrives while it is still useful, rubric-aligned and specific enough that a student knows which decision to make differently next time — a grade with no repair instruction is an administrative act rather than a teaching one. And being findable: I keep an active presence in the LMS, in Canvas and BrightSpace, so a question asked on Tuesday is answered on Tuesday.

Retention work is done in partnership rather than alone. In my current role I work with the Learning Support Coordinator to identify students at risk of non-completion from attendance, submission and performance signals, and to put support in place early.

I came to Australian higher education as an international student myself. That is not a credential, but it does affect what I notice.

Coordination

Coordination, quality and accreditation

In 2025 I ran a new study centre under a third-party agreement between the Education Centre of Australia and the University of Tasmania. That meant academic operations for a first intake of 80+ students: timetabling across two semesters, coordination with enrolments, admissions, recruitment and student services, academic risk management, and the recruitment, induction and mentoring of five or more casual academics across two intakes and two campuses.

I then prepared documentation and coordinated committee meetings for ACS accreditation of the ICT program, granted across both study centres in October 2025. That work sat inside TEQSA and ESOS requirements, including the online-study and face-to-face load conditions that apply to student visa holders.

I also support unit coordinators in refining assessment tasks and rubrics, and hold coordination responsibility for ICT units at both study centres.
